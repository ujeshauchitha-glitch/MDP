# Status report: overview of the work so far (2026-10-17)

*Plain-language summary. Everything described here is a computer simulation (ngspice with
the open sky130 chip process, plus Python); nothing has been built or measured on real
hardware. For the technical detail see [`../STATUS.md`](../STATUS.md) (one page) and
[`../afe-architecture.md`](../afe-architecture.md) (full record).*

## 1. What we are building

A small analog circuit (the "front end") for a wearable sensor that shines light on the skin
and reads the tiny current produced by a photodiode. It has two jobs:

1. **Read the pulse (PPG).** The slowly changing light level carries your heartbeat. The
   signal is tiny (picoamps to nanoamps) on top of a much larger steady current.
2. **Check the light is genuine (anti-spoofing).** The device flickers its own LED with a
   secret pattern of 48 on/off "chips" every 100 µs. It checks that exactly that pattern
   comes back. Someone trying to fake a heartbeat with their own light source would not
   know the pattern.

The two jobs need very different circuits (very high gain and low speed for the pulse; lower
gain and high speed for the pattern), and they have to share one photodiode.

## 2. The design as it stands now

![architecture](../architecture.svg)

* **Slow path (pulse):** connects straight to the photodiode. A large resistor network turns
  the tiny current into a voltage (1 GΩ, with 100 MΩ and 10 MΩ ranges that are picked
  automatically for bright vs dim signals).
* **Steady-current canceller:** a digital loop measures the steady current and subtracts it
  (otherwise it would swamp the pulse). It locks in about 90 ms after power-up.
* **Fast path (pattern):** attached to the same photodiode through a capacitor, so only
  the fast flicker passes. A mirror and a second amplifier turn it into a voltage that a
  correlator compares with the secret pattern.

## 3. The results, in plain words

| Question | Answer | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| How dim a signal can the pattern check still handle? | needs a steady photocurrent of **about 24 nA** (with the flicker at 2 % of it); 49 nA at 1 %; 97 nA at 0.5 % | below that the check cannot be trusted. The first plan expected it to work down to 10 nA, so this is a **shortfall**, worst for people with low blood flow or dark skin |
| Does it still work when conditions change? | yes: across the hot / cold / fast / slow chip corners and ±10 % supply voltage the figure stays **21–29 nA** | robust in simulation |
| Does the pulse path read correctly? | gain stays within 1.5 % of 1 GΩ everywhere; recovered pulse is 99.4 % of the true amplitude | good |
| Does supply noise get in? | it did badly at first; after two fixes it is **24 to 13 000 times** better | solved except for fast ripple above ~500 kHz |
| Is it stable? | loop phase margins 62–80° with the low-power settings | good margins |
| How much power? | **61–74 µW** (analog only) with the low-power settings; 241–299 µW with the original settings | meets the original 87 µW goal, digital circuits not counted |

## 4. What we got wrong at the start (and fixed)

The first design was only calculated by hand. Running it showed many problems:

* The first netlist did not even load (wrong device syntax).
* The "1 GΩ resistor" made from transistors varied from 0.8 MΩ to 120 GΩ with temperature
  and chip variation, so it was replaced by a real resistor network.
* A "regulated cascode" input stage that was supposed to isolate the photodiode never
  actually regulated; the simpler stage is better.
* The plan to share one input between both paths through a mirror made the pulse path
  about 29 times noisier; the pulse path now connects directly.
* At 10 % lower supply voltage the design broke (the output stages ran out of headroom);
  lowering the mid-level from 1.65 V to 1.4 V fixed it.
* The supply rejection problem was traced (after three wrong guesses) to the transistors'
  "body effect", and fixed by wiring their wells to quiet nodes.
* A 1 GΩ setting clips any pulse above about 250 pA, so three ranges were added.

## 5. What is wrong or risky right now (honest list)

1. **Low-signal shortfall:** the pattern check needs at least ~24 nA of steady current.
2. **Security is weaker than the early documents claimed.** The detector as first designed
   accepts a random fake 16 % of the time; an added check fixes that. But a fast
   "relay" attacker who watches each flicker and copies it back within ~130 ns still gets
   through, and a simple shift-register pattern generator can be predicted after 64 chips.
   A proper cryptographic generator and a decision about relay attacks are needed.
3. **The steady-current canceller dominates the pulse noise** (85–95 %). A resistor
   in its source cuts that noise 5–8x in simulation; a real digital-to-analog converter has
   not been designed.
4. **Assumptions in the model:** reference currents, resistors and the supply are ideal;
   the transistor noise model has not been compared with real silicon; no layout (a 50 pF
   coupling capacitor alone is about a third of the available tile).
5. Digital power and area are not included.

## 6. How we know the numbers are right (the audit)

We re-checked the work: the detection simulator matches a hand formula to 0.1 %; the noise
units match a 1 kΩ resistor exactly; the original runner reproduces its first results; and
we found and corrected two documentation errors plus stale entries. One simulator setting
(very tight tolerances) was found to produce impossible noise values in some cases; all
reported results use the safe default.

## 8. Glossary

* **TIA:** transimpedance amplifier: turns a tiny current into a voltage.
* **PPG:** photoplethysmography: the light-based pulse signal.
* **I_DC:** the steady photodiode current. **m:** the fraction of it that the flicker uses.
* **Pd / Pfa:** probability of correctly accepting the real pattern / of wrongly accepting noise.
  We require 99.9 % and one in 100 million.
* **PVT:** process, voltage, temperature variation. **PSRR:** how well the circuit ignores supply noise.
* **Servo:** the loop that cancels the steady current. **DAC:** digital-to-analog converter.
* **Relay attack:** an attacker who copies the flicker back quickly instead of guessing it.
